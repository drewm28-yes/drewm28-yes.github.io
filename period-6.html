Period 6

The Gilded Age: Industry, Labor & the West

The decades between 1865 and 1898 witnessed the transformation of the United States into an industrial giant — and the profound human costs of that transformation. Railroad networks and steel mills created extraordinary wealth concentrated in the hands of "Robber Barons" like Rockefeller, Carnegie, and Vanderbilt, who wielded economic power that dwarfed state governments. Workers responded with the nation's first sustained labor movement. In the American West, the completion of the transcontinental railroad accelerated both white settlement and the final destruction of Plains Indian cultures. The Supreme Court's ruling in Plessy v. Ferguson (1896) constitutionally sanctioned racial segregation, betraying Reconstruction's promise for another six decades.

Exam Focus
Period 6 is 13% of the exam. High-yield topics: the causes and consequences of industrialization, the Gilded Age's response to corporate power (or lack thereof), the destruction of Plains Indian cultures, and Plessy v. Ferguson as the constitutional foundation for Jim Crow segregation.
  • 1869
    Transcontinental Railroad Completed
    The joining of the Central Pacific and Union Pacific lines at Promontory Summit, Utah, connected the continent — built largely by Chinese and Irish immigrant laborers under brutal conditions. The railroad collapsed transportation costs, opened western markets, devastated Plains Indian buffalo economies, and accelerated the destruction of Native cultures by flooding the West with settlers.
    WXTMIGGEO
  • 1875–76
    Battle of Little Bighorn & Native American Resistance
    Lakota, Northern Cheyenne, and Arapaho warriors under Sitting Bull and Crazy Horse annihilated Lt. Col. George Custer's 7th Cavalry at Little Bighorn — the last major victory of Native American resistance. The Dawes Act (1887) attempted to "civilize" Native peoples by dividing tribal lands into individual allotments, destroying communal land ownership and ultimately transferring 90 million acres to white settlers.
    GEOMIGPCE
  • 1877
    Great Railroad Strike of 1877
    America's first nationwide labor strike paralyzed railroads across the country after wage cuts provoked a spontaneous uprising. President Hayes deployed federal troops to suppress the strike, killing dozens of workers. The strike revealed both the enormous power of industrial capital and the emerging power of organized labor — galvanizing the labor movement and demonstrating that class conflict was now a permanent feature of American life.
    WXTPCESOC
  • 1879–89
    The Gilded Age: Robber Barons & Social Darwinism
    John D. Rockefeller's Standard Oil trust, Andrew Carnegie's steel empire, and Cornelius Vanderbilt's railroad network represented the concentration of wealth on a scale previously unimaginable in America. Social Darwinism — Herbert Spencer's application of evolutionary theory to economics — provided the ideological justification for inequality: the wealthy were "fit," the poor were "unfit." Carnegie's "Gospel of Wealth" (1889) argued that the rich had an obligation to give back — but not to change the system producing inequality.
    WXTSOCNAT
  • 1886
    American Federation of Labor Founded
    Samuel Gompers founded the AFL as a federation of skilled craft unions focused on "bread and butter" demands — wages, hours, and working conditions — rather than political revolution. The AFL's pragmatic approach contrasted with the broader, more radical Knights of Labor. The Haymarket Affair (1886) — a bomb at a Chicago labor rally killing seven police officers — devastated the labor movement through association with anarchism and foreign radicalism.
    WXTSOCPCE
  • 1890
    Sherman Antitrust Act
    Congress passed the first federal law regulating corporate monopolies, prohibiting "every contract, combination, or conspiracy in restraint of trade." However, the law was initially applied more against labor unions than corporations — the Supreme Court's ruling in United States v. E.C. Knight (1895) gutted its effectiveness against manufacturing monopolies, leaving corporations largely free to consolidate.
    PCEWXT
  • 1896
    Plessy v. Ferguson — "Separate But Equal"
    The Supreme Court upheld Louisiana's Separate Car Act, ruling that "separate but equal" facilities were constitutional under the 14th Amendment — providing the constitutional foundation for Jim Crow segregation throughout the South. Justice Harlan's famous dissent argued "the Constitution is color-blind." Plessy was not overturned until Brown v. Board of Education in 1954.
    PCESOCNAT
MAGPIES Framework

Thematic Breakdown

E
Economy & Technology (WXT)
The Second Industrial Revolution & its discontents
▼

The Second Industrial Revolution (1870–1914) transformed the United States from an agricultural nation into the world's leading industrial power. Steel, oil, railroads, and telecommunications created integrated national markets and vast corporate wealth. Vertical integration (Carnegie's steel — controlling everything from iron ore to finished product) and horizontal integration (Rockefeller's Standard Oil trust — controlling 90% of U.S. oil refining) concentrated unprecedented economic power. The laissez-faire ideology opposed government regulation, though Populist and Progressive movements would challenge this in the next period.

Evidence: Standard Oil · Carnegie Steel · Transcontinental Railroad · Sherman Antitrust Act · Social Darwinism · Gospel of Wealth · Panic of 1873 and 1893
S
Social Structures (SOC)
Labor, immigration, and racial hierarchy
▼

Gilded Age industrialization produced a new American working class — immigrant, diverse, and often desperate. Millions of immigrants from southern and eastern Europe (Italians, Poles, Jews, Russians) flooded into industrial cities, living in overcrowded tenements and working 12-hour days in dangerous conditions. The Chinese Exclusion Act (1882) — the first law specifically barring immigrants by race — demonstrated that nativist anxiety about cultural change was producing racial restriction on immigration. Meanwhile, in the South, Plessy v. Ferguson's "separate but equal" doctrine constitutionally entrenched racial segregation in every sphere of life.

Evidence: Chinese Exclusion Act · Tenement conditions · Knights of Labor · AFL · Plessy v. Ferguson · Booker T. Washington vs. W.E.B. Du Bois (emerging debate)
M
Migration & Settlement (MIG)
The closing of the frontier & Native dispossession
▼

Frederick Jackson Turner's "Frontier Thesis" (1893) argued that the frontier experience had shaped the American character — and lamented that the 1890 census had declared the frontier "closed." In reality, the "closing" of the frontier meant the completion of a catastrophic dispossession of Native peoples. The transcontinental railroad brought settlers flooding into the Great Plains; systematic buffalo slaughter destroyed the Plains Indians' food base; and the Dawes Act (1887) broke up communal tribal lands. By 1890, the Wounded Knee Massacre — in which U.S. troops killed over 250 Lakota Sioux — symbolized the final destruction of organized Native American resistance.

Evidence: Transcontinental Railroad · Battle of Little Bighorn · Dawes Act · Turner's Frontier Thesis · Wounded Knee Massacre · Homestead Act
Historical Thinking Skill

Skill Spotlight: CCOT

Prompt: Analyze continuities and changes in the federal government's relationship to the economy from 1865 to 1898.

Continuity
What Persisted?
  • Laissez-faire ideology dominated economic policy throughout — government largely deferred to business
  • Federal land grants continued subsidizing railroad expansion with minimal regulation
  • Courts consistently sided with corporations over labor unions in disputes
Change
What Changed & When?
  • (1877) First federal use of troops against labor strikers established a precedent
  • (1887) Interstate Commerce Act created first federal regulatory agency (ICC)
  • (1890) Sherman Antitrust Act — first attempt at federal anti-monopoly legislation
  • (1890s) Populist movement began demanding more radical government intervention
Model Thesis

"While laissez-faire ideology kept the federal government largely out of economic regulation throughout 1865–1898, the excesses of Gilded Age industrialization produced growing demands for intervention — culminating in the Sherman Antitrust Act and the Interstate Commerce Act — though these early measures were ultimately ineffective, setting the stage for the Progressive Era's more robust regulatory state."

Primary Source Practice

HIPP Analysis: Andrew Carnegie, "Gospel of Wealth" (1889)

"The problem of our age is the proper administration of wealth, that the ties of brotherhood may still bind together the rich and poor in harmonious relationship. The conditions of human life have not only been changed, but revolutionized, within the past few hundred years… The contrast between the palace of the millionaire and the cottage of the laborer with us to-day measures the change which has come with civilization. This change, however, is not to be deplored, but welcomed as highly beneficial… The 'good old times' were not good old times. Neither master nor servant was as well situated then as to-day."

— Andrew Carnegie, "Wealth," North American Review, June 1889
📋
Document Context
Andrew Carnegie (1835–1919) was a Scottish immigrant who built Carnegie Steel into the world's largest steel corporation, becoming one of America's wealthiest men. His "Gospel of Wealth" argued that the rich had an obligation to use their surplus wealth for public benefit — but also that industrial capitalism's inequality was natural, beneficial, and divinely ordained.

CB Key Concepts
Period 6 · 1865–1898
Key Concept 6.1
Technological advances, large-scale production, and the opening of new markets connected the U.S. to a global economy and spurred urbanization and immigration.
Key Concept 6.2
The migrations that occurred in this period, along with the new communities they formed, affected the culture of the U.S.
Key Concept 6.3
The Gilded Age produced significant cultural and intellectual debates over power, religion, politics, and culture.
Key Terms
Period 6 Must-Know
Social DarwinismHerbert Spencer's application of Darwin's evolution to economics — justified inequality by arguing the wealthy were "fit" survivors. Used to oppose government welfare programs.
Vertical IntegrationControlling all stages of production (Carnegie's steel: iron ore mines → furnaces → finished steel) — eliminated middlemen and maximized profit.
Horizontal IntegrationBuying out competitors in the same industry (Rockefeller's Standard Oil controlled 90% of U.S. refining) — created monopolistic trusts.
Plessy v. Ferguson"Separate but equal" (1896) — provided constitutional sanction for racial segregation throughout the South for 58 years until Brown v. Board.
Dawes Act (1887)Broke up tribal communal lands into individual allotments — transferred 90 million acres to white settlers and destroyed Native communal culture.
Populist MovementAgrarian movement demanding railroad regulation, silver coinage, and direct democracy — challenged laissez-faire ideology and previewed Progressive Era reforms.
Exam Quick Tips
Plessy = Jim Crow FoundationKnow Plessy v. Ferguson (1896) as the constitutional basis for segregation, and Brown v. Board (1954) as its overturning. The 58-year gap between them is the story of Period 8.
Robber Baron vs. Captain of IndustryA classic compare/contrast: "Robber Baron" = exploitative monopolist; "Captain of Industry" = productive builder of national wealth. Know how contemporaries used both terms.
Labor Movement LimitsKnow why the labor movement struggled: courts sided with corporations, federal troops broke strikes, and Social Darwinism provided ideological cover for inequality.
