APUSH Historical Period

Period 3

Revolution, Independence & the New Republic

The era from 1754 to 1800 stands as one of the most consequential in American history. What began as a costly imperial war over frontier territory escalated into a full-scale ideological rupture between Britain and its colonies. Enlightenment ideals of natural rights, self-governance, and popular sovereignty provided colonists with a framework to challenge parliamentary authority. The Revolution not only produced national independence but forced a generation of founders to confront the deepest questions of republican government: Who holds power? How should it be constrained? And whose liberty truly mattered?

Exam Focus
Period 3 is 12% of the exam. Expect DBQ prompts on Patriot motivations or limits of Revolutionary change. Common LEQ: To what extent was the Revolution "revolutionary" for different groups?
  • 1754–63
    French & Indian War
    Britain's victory drove France from North America but left massive war debt, setting the stage for colonial taxation. The war also gave colonial militias like George Washington crucial military experience.
    WORMIGGEO
  • 1763–73
    Imperial Taxation Crisis
    The Proclamation of 1763, Stamp Act, Townshend Acts, and Tea Act provoked organized colonial resistance under "No Taxation Without Representation," challenging Parliament's claim of virtual representation.
    PCEWXTNAT
  • 1776
    Declaration of Independence
    Thomas Jefferson grounded independence in Enlightenment natural rights philosophy — Locke's ideas of life, liberty, and property — articulating a universal claim about human equality that became a contested standard for generations.
    PCENATSOC
  • 1777–83
    Revolutionary War & Treaty of Paris
    Victory at Saratoga (1777) brought France in as an ally. Britain's surrender at Yorktown (1781) ended the conflict. The Treaty of Paris (1783) recognized American sovereignty east of the Mississippi River.
    WORPCE
  • 1781–87
    Articles of Confederation & Failures
    The new nation's first constitution created a weak central government that could not tax, regulate commerce, or raise an army. Shays' Rebellion (1786) exposed its fatal weakness and galvanized the Constitutional Convention.
    PCEWXT
  • 1787–91
    Constitution, Ratification & Bill of Rights
    The Constitutional Convention produced a federal system with separation of powers. Federalists (Hamilton, Madison, Jay — the Federalist Papers) debated Anti-Federalists. The Bill of Rights (1791) secured individual liberties.
    PCENATSOC
  • 1798–1800
    Alien & Sedition Acts; "Revolution of 1800"
    The Alien and Sedition Acts criminalized government criticism. Jefferson responded with the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions. Jefferson's peaceful election victory in 1800 set a crucial democratic precedent.
    PCENAT
MAGPIES Framework

Thematic Breakdown

P
Politics & Power (PCE)
Most heavily tested theme in Period 3
▼

Period 3 is fundamentally a story about who holds power and by what authority. Colonial resistance challenged Parliamentary sovereignty using Enlightenment arguments that government must derive consent from the governed. Post-Revolutionary debates over the Articles and Constitution continued this argument domestically — between those fearing centralized tyranny (Anti-Federalists) and those fearing governmental paralysis (Federalists).

Evidence to Use: Stamp Act Congress · Declaration of Independence · Articles of Confederation · Federalist No. 51 · Alien & Sedition Acts · Virginia & Kentucky Resolutions · Election of 1800
A
America in the World (WOR)
Foreign policy, diplomacy & global context
▼

The Revolution cannot be understood without its global context. The French and Indian War was the American theater of a global conflict. France's military intervention after Saratoga was motivated by strategic rivalry with Britain, not sympathy for republican ideals. The French Revolution forced Washington's Proclamation of Neutrality (1793). The XYZ Affair showed how difficult neutrality was in practice. Washington's Farewell Address warned against "entangling alliances."

Evidence to Use: French alliance (1778) · Treaty of Paris (1783) · Proclamation of Neutrality · XYZ Affair · Washington's Farewell Address
I
American Identity (NAT)
Who is an American? Who is included?
▼

The Revolution produced a new national identity centered on liberty — but exposed deep contradictions. Enslaved people, inspired by revolutionary rhetoric, petitioned for freedom and some northern states began gradual abolition. Yet the Constitution entrenched slavery through the Three-Fifths Compromise. "Republican motherhood" offered women a limited but real expansion of civic importance. Native Americans universally lost ground regardless of which side they had supported.

Evidence to Use: Declaration of Independence · Three-Fifths Compromise · Abigail Adams "Remember the Ladies" · Republican Motherhood · Northwest Ordinance
Historical Thinking Skill

Skill Spotlight: CCOT

Prompt: Analyze continuities and changes in the American political relationship with Britain, 1740–1800.

Continuity
What Stayed the Same?
  • English common law continued to shape American legal systems
  • Anglo-American cultural and economic ties persisted post-1783
  • Fear of concentrated executive power shaped both colonial and constitutional government
Change
What Changed & When?
  • (1763–1776) Salutary neglect replaced by active imperial taxation
  • (1776) Subject status replaced by independent republican citizenship
  • (1787–91) No constitution replaced by federal constitutional republic
Model Thesis

"While the American Revolution fundamentally transformed the political relationship between the colonies and Britain from imperial subjugation to sovereign independence, significant continuities in legal tradition, cultural identity, and Atlantic economic ties persisted beyond 1783, demonstrating that the Revolution was as much a conservative preservation of English liberties as a radical rupture."

Primary Source Practice

HIPP Analysis: Thomas Paine, Common Sense (1776)

"Society in every state is a blessing, but Government, even in its best state, is but a necessary evil; in its worst state an intolerable one… O! ye that love mankind! Ye that dare oppose not only the tyranny but the tyrant, stand forth! Every spot of the old world is overrun with oppression. Freedom hath been hunted round the globe… O! receive the fugitive, and prepare in time an asylum for mankind."

— Thomas Paine, Common Sense, January 1776  ·  Philadelphia  ·  100,000+ copies distributed
📋
Document Context
Thomas Paine was a British-born pamphleteer who recently emigrated to Philadelphia. Written six months before the Declaration of Independence, Common Sense was the first publication to argue publicly for complete separation from Britain, using plain language to reach ordinary colonists.

CB Key Concepts
Period 3 · 1754–1800
Key Concept 3.1
British attempts to assert tighter control led to a colonial independence movement and the Revolutionary War.
Key Concept 3.2
The Revolution's democratic ideals inspired new experiments with different forms of government.
Key Concept 3.3
Migration and competition over resources, boundaries, and trade intensified conflicts among peoples and nations.
Key Terms
Period 3 Must-Know
Salutary NeglectBritain's lax enforcement of trade laws before 1763, allowing colonial self-governance to flourish.
Virtual RepresentationBritish argument that Parliament represented all subjects — rejected by colonists demanding actual representation.
Natural RightsEnlightenment (Locke) concept of inherent rights to life, liberty, and property that no government can revoke.
Three-Fifths CompromiseCounted enslaved persons as 3/5 of a person for representation — entrenching slavery in the Constitution.
Northwest Ordinance (1787)Banned slavery north of the Ohio River; established the statehood process — crucial precedent for sectional debates.
Republican MotherhoodPost-Revolution ideal valuing women's role raising virtuous citizens — limited but real civic expansion.
Exam Quick Tips
Saratoga = Turning PointAlways explain why: it convinced France to formally enter the war — that cause-effect connection is what examiners want.
Revolution Was NOT Fully RevolutionaryIt was politically radical but socially conservative — didn't free enslaved people or give women political rights. Use as your complexity argument.
Election of 1800 = PrecedentFirst peaceful transfer of power between parties — frame as globally unprecedented and constitutionally significant.
