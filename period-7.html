Period 7

Progressivism, World Wars & the New Deal

Period 7 spans the most turbulent half-century in American history — from the reform-minded Progressive Era through two catastrophic world wars and the Great Depression. Progressives challenged Gilded Age laissez-faire with the conviction that expert government could regulate corporate power and improve urban life. World War I thrust the United States onto the global stage as an emerging superpower. The Jazz Age's prosperity masked deep structural economic weaknesses that produced the Great Depression, which in turn generated the New Deal — the most dramatic expansion of federal power in American history. World War II completed America's transformation into a true global superpower while exposing the profound contradictions of fighting fascism abroad while practicing racial segregation at home.

Exam Focus
Period 7 is the single most heavily tested period at 17%. Know the Progressive Era's key reforms, the debates over U.S. involvement in both World Wars, the causes and consequences of the Great Depression, and the New Deal's expansion of federal power. FDR's First Inaugural is a classic DBQ document.
  • 1898
    Spanish-American War & American Imperialism
    The "splendid little war" produced an American empire — Cuba (nominally independent, actually a protectorate), Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines. The debate over the Philippines was fierce: anti-imperialists like Mark Twain and Andrew Carnegie argued empire contradicted American democratic principles. The Philippine-American War (1899–1902) — brutal, racist, and long — foreshadowed 20th-century counterinsurgency conflicts.
    WORNATPCE
  • 1900–20
    The Progressive Era
    Progressive reformers — journalists (muckrakers like Upton Sinclair, Ida Tarbell), social workers (Jane Addams), and politicians (Theodore Roosevelt, Woodrow Wilson) — challenged Gilded Age laissez-faire. Key achievements: 16th Amendment (federal income tax), 17th Amendment (direct election of senators), 18th Amendment (Prohibition), 19th Amendment (women's suffrage, 1920), Pure Food and Drug Act, Sherman Antitrust enforcement, and the Federal Reserve Act (1913).
    PCESOCWXT
  • 1917–18
    U.S. Enters World War I
    Wilson's reluctant declaration of war was triggered by German unrestricted submarine warfare and the Zimmermann Telegram. The war required massive mobilization — the War Industries Board coordinated the economy; the Espionage and Sedition Acts suppressed dissent; and 370,000 Black Americans served in a segregated military. Wilson's Fourteen Points and the League of Nations vision shaped the peace — but the Senate rejected the Treaty of Versailles, leaving the U.S. in isolation.
    WORPCENAT
  • 1920s
    The Roaring Twenties & Cultural Tensions
    The 1920s were a decade of surface prosperity and deep cultural conflict. Mass consumer culture, jazz, radio, and automobiles transformed American life. The Harlem Renaissance produced an outpouring of Black artistic achievement. But the KKK revived dramatically, immigration restriction (Immigration Act of 1924) imposed ethnic quotas, and Prohibition drove alcohol underground while enriching organized crime. The decade's prosperity rested on unsustainable stock speculation.
    SOCNATWXT
  • 1929–33
    The Great Depression
    The stock market crash of October 1929 — caused by overspeculation, underconsumption, agricultural collapse, and weak banking regulation — triggered the worst economic catastrophe in American history. By 1933, 25% of workers were unemployed; 5,000 banks had failed; and Hoover's response of voluntary action and "associationalism" had catastrophically failed. The Dust Bowl combined with economic collapse to displace hundreds of thousands from the Great Plains.
    WXTSOCGEO
  • 1933–38
    FDR's New Deal
    Franklin Roosevelt's New Deal represented the most dramatic expansion of federal government in American history. The "Three Rs" — Relief (FERA, CCC), Recovery (NRA, AAA), and Reform (FDIC, SEC, Social Security Act of 1935, Wagner Act) — created the modern American welfare state and regulatory framework. The New Deal created a Democratic coalition that would dominate politics until the 1960s, though critics from both left (too timid) and right (socialism) challenged it.
    PCEWXTSOC
  • 1941–45
    World War II & the Home Front
    Japan's attack on Pearl Harbor (December 7, 1941) ended American neutrality. World War II required total mobilization: the War Production Board converted industry to war production; 16 million Americans served in the military; and women and minorities entered the workforce en masse. Executive Order 9066 forcibly relocated 110,000 Japanese Americans to internment camps — one of the most shameful civil liberties violations in American history. The use of atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki ended the war and began the nuclear age.

MAGPIES Framework

Thematic Breakdown

P
Politics & Power (PCE)
Progressive reform, executive power, & the New Deal state
▼

Period 7 witnesses the dramatic expansion of federal power in response to industrial capitalism's failures. Progressive Presidents Roosevelt (TR), Taft, and Wilson used executive authority to regulate corporations, break up trusts, and pass constitutional amendments expanding democracy. The New Deal's 100 Days produced an unprecedented legislative torrent creating agencies, programs, and regulations that built the modern administrative state. FDR's "court-packing" plan (1937) — attempting to add justices to a Supreme Court blocking New Deal legislation — failed but pressured the Court to sustain the New Deal's constitutionality.

Evidence: Progressive Amendments (16th–19th) · Federal Reserve Act · Sherman Antitrust enforcement · New Deal agencies (FDIC, SEC, SSA) · Wagner Act · FDR's fireside chats · Court-packing controversy
A
America in the World (WOR)
Isolationism vs. global engagement, 1898–1945
▼

Period 7 marks America's transformation from continental power to global hegemon. The Spanish-American War (1898) produced an overseas empire. Wilson's World War I intervention — justified as making "the world safe for democracy" — was followed by Senate rejection of the League of Nations and a retreat into isolationism during the 1920s and 1930s. But the Great Depression's global devastation, Nazi expansionism, and Pearl Harbor ultimately forced American engagement. World War II ended with America as the world's dominant economic and military power, holding nuclear weapons — fundamentally transforming the global order.

Evidence: Spanish-American War · Open Door Policy · Wilson's Fourteen Points · League of Nations rejection · Neutrality Acts (1935–37) · Lend-Lease Act · Pearl Harbor · Manhattan Project
S
Social Structures (SOC)
Migration, identity, and the limits of reform
▼

Period 7 produced dramatic social transformations. The Great Migration (beginning c. 1915) brought hundreds of thousands of Black Americans from the rural South to northern industrial cities, creating the conditions for the Harlem Renaissance and the NAACP's growing influence. Women won the vote (19th Amendment, 1920). But the period also saw intense racial violence — the Red Summer of 1919 (race riots in 25 cities), the revived KKK, and the Japanese American internment during WWII. The New Deal itself often excluded Black Americans through discriminatory application — the Social Security Act initially exempted domestic and agricultural workers, categories dominated by Black Americans.

Evidence: Great Migration · Harlem Renaissance · 19th Amendment · Immigration Act of 1924 · Red Summer 1919 · Japanese internment (EO 9066) · Double V Campaign · New Deal racial exclusions
Historical Thinking Skill

Skill Spotlight: CCOT

Prompt: Analyze continuities and changes in the role of the federal government in the American economy from 1890 to 1945.

Continuity
What Persisted?
  • Capitalism remained the foundational economic system throughout — neither Progressivism nor the New Deal replaced it
  • Business interests continued to exert significant political influence despite reform efforts
  • Courts continued to use the Constitution to limit regulation (until the "switch in time" of 1937)
Change
What Changed & When?
  • (1890–1920) Federal government began regulating railroads, trusts, food, and banking
  • (1913) Federal Reserve Act created central banking; income tax (16th Amendment) established
  • (1929–33) Great Depression destroyed laissez-faire consensus
  • (1933–38) New Deal created the modern regulatory and welfare state (SSA, FDIC, SEC, Wagner Act)
Model Thesis

"While capitalism remained the foundation of the American economy throughout 1890–1945, the federal government's role transformed fundamentally — from the Progressive Era's tentative regulatory experiments to the New Deal's creation of the modern welfare and administrative state — as the failures of industrial capitalism produced successively greater demands for government intervention that permanently expanded Washington's economic authority."

Primary Source Practice

HIPP Analysis: FDR's First Inaugural Address (1933)

"This is preeminently the time to speak the truth, the whole truth, frankly and boldly. Nor need we shrink from honestly facing conditions in our country today. This great Nation will endure, as it has endured, will revive and will prosper. So, first of all, let me assert my firm belief that the only thing we have to fear is fear itself — nameless, unreasoning, unjustified terror which paralyzes needed efforts to convert retreat into advance… The money changers have fled from their high seats in the temple of our civilization. We may now restore that temple to the ancient truths."

— Franklin D. Roosevelt, First Inaugural Address, March 4, 1933  ·  25% unemployment; banking system on verge of collapse
📋
Document Context
Franklin Roosevelt delivered this address on March 4, 1933, with 25% of Americans unemployed, 5,000 banks failed, and the nation in profound economic and psychological crisis. The "hundred days" that followed produced the most sweeping legislative agenda in American history. FDR's famous phrase "the only thing we have to fear is fear itself" was designed to restore public confidence and legitimize active government intervention.

Period 7 · 1890–1945
Key Concept 7.1
Growth expanded opportunity, while economic instability led to new efforts to reform U.S. society and its economic system.
Key Concept 7.2
Innovations in communications and technology contributed to mass culture growth, while significant changes in migration patterns occurred.
Key Concept 7.3
Participation in global conflicts propelled the U.S. into a position of international power while renewing domestic debates over its proper role in the world.
Key Terms
Period 7 Must-Know
MuckrakersInvestigative journalists exposing corporate and political corruption — Ida Tarbell (Standard Oil), Upton Sinclair (The Jungle), Jacob Riis (tenements).
New Deal "Three Rs"Relief (immediate aid), Recovery (economic revitalization), Reform (structural changes to prevent future depression) — the framework of FDR's New Deal programs.
Great Migration600,000+ Black Americans moved from the Jim Crow South to northern cities (1910–1930), driven by racial violence and economic opportunity in war industries.
IsolationismForeign policy of avoiding European entanglements — dominant in the 1920s–30s until Pearl Harbor forced abandonment.
Executive Order 9066FDR's 1942 order forcibly relocating 110,000 Japanese Americans to internment camps — a major civil liberties violation upheld by Korematsu v. U.S. (1944).
Wagner Act (1935)Guaranteed workers' right to organize and collectively bargain — the most significant pro-labor legislation in American history, enabling CIO mass unionization.
Exam Quick Tips
Period 7 = 17% of the ExamThe highest-weight period. Know the New Deal programs cold: SSA, FDIC, CCC, WPA, Wagner Act. Know which were declared unconstitutional and why FDR proposed court-packing.
Depression Causes Are Multi-FactorNever give a single cause. Know: stock speculation, under-consumption, agricultural weakness, banking fragility, Smoot-Hawley tariff, and global interconnection.
New Deal ≠ Full RecoveryFor complexity: the New Deal reduced suffering but didn't end the Depression — WWII military spending produced full employment. This is the exam's favorite nuance.
