APUSH Historical Period

Period 9

Conservative Ascendancy, Globalization & the New Century

From Reagan's election in 1980 to the present, American history has been defined by two overarching transformations: the conservative political revolution that dismantled key elements of the New Deal and Great Society welfare state, and the acceleration of globalization driven by digital technology, free trade, and the end of the Cold War. The Soviet Union's collapse in 1991 left the United States as the world's sole superpower — a unipolar moment that ended catastrophically on September 11, 2001. The wars in Afghanistan and Iraq, the 2008 financial crisis, rising inequality, and the growing polarization of American politics constitute the unresolved tensions that students must analyze with historical thinking skills rather than partisan frameworks.

Key Concepts & Major Events

🏛️
Exam Focus
Period 9 is only 5% of the exam — the same as Period 1. Focus on understanding Reagan's ideological shift as a break from the New Deal consensus, the Cold War's end and its global consequences, and September 11's impact on civil liberties and foreign policy. The College Board focuses on causation and contextualization here, not detailed memorization.
  • 1980–88
    Reagan Revolution & Conservative Ascendancy
    Ronald Reagan's 1980 landslide victory marked the triumph of a new conservatism that had been building since Goldwater's 1964 defeat. "Reaganomics" — supply-side tax cuts, deregulation, and reduced domestic spending — represented an explicit repudiation of the New Deal liberal consensus. Reagan broke the PATCO air traffic controllers' strike (1981), signaling a decisive shift in labor-management power. His massive defense buildup — the "Star Wars" Strategic Defense Initiative — combined with economic pressure helped collapse the Soviet economy. Reagan's rhetoric ("government is the problem") fundamentally shifted the terms of American political debate.
    PCEWXTNAT
  • 1989–91
    End of the Cold War
    The fall of the Berlin Wall (November 9, 1989) symbolized the collapse of Communist regimes across Eastern Europe. The Soviet Union itself dissolved in December 1991, ending forty-five years of Cold War and leaving the United States as the world's sole military and economic superpower. President George H.W. Bush's Gulf War (1991) — assembling an international coalition to expel Iraq from Kuwait — demonstrated American military supremacy and the possibilities of post-Cold War multilateralism.
    WORPCENAT
  • 1990s
    Clinton Era: Globalization & the Digital Revolution
    Bill Clinton's "New Democrat" triangulation — NAFTA (1993), welfare reform, and balanced budgets — accepted key elements of Reagan's conservative framework while defending social programs. The 1990s saw explosive growth in digital technology and the internet, transforming communication, commerce, and culture. The dot-com boom produced extraordinary wealth while simultaneously accelerating the deindustrialization of working-class communities. Clinton's impeachment (1998) over the Monica Lewinsky affair demonstrated the increasing weaponization of political conflict.
    WXTSOCPCE
  • 2001
    September 11 & the War on Terror
    Al-Qaeda's coordinated attacks on the World Trade Center and Pentagon killed nearly 3,000 people — the deadliest attack on American soil since Pearl Harbor. The USA PATRIOT Act dramatically expanded government surveillance powers; the Department of Homeland Security was created; and the Authorization for Use of Military Force gave the president broad war-making authority. The subsequent invasions of Afghanistan (2001) and Iraq (2003) — the latter based on faulty WMD intelligence — defined American foreign policy for the next two decades.
    WORPCENAT
  • 2008
    Financial Crisis & the Obama Era
    The collapse of the housing bubble triggered the worst financial crisis since the Great Depression — producing bank bailouts (TARP), the auto industry rescue, and the $787 billion American Recovery and Reinvestment Act. Barack Obama's historic election as the first Black president seemed to embody America's democratic promise; the Affordable Care Act (2010) represented the largest expansion of health coverage since Medicare. But the Tea Party's rise demonstrated the depth of conservative backlash to both Obama's presidency and the financial crisis.
    WXTPCENAT
  • 2010s–20s
    Rising Polarization & Social Movements
    The decade following 2010 saw accelerating political polarization, economic inequality, and social movement activism. Black Lives Matter (founded 2013) challenged systemic police violence and racial inequality. The #MeToo movement (2017) confronted sexual harassment and assault. Rising economic inequality — the top 1% accumulating a growing share of national wealth while wages stagnated for working-class Americans — produced political movements across the spectrum, from Occupy Wall Street to the Tea Party to the Sanders campaign.
    SOCPCEWXT
  • 2020s
    COVID-19 & Contemporary Challenges
    The COVID-19 pandemic (beginning 2020) killed over one million Americans — the deadliest public health crisis since the 1918 influenza pandemic — while exposing deep racial and economic inequalities in access to healthcare and economic resilience. Climate change, immigration, and ongoing debates over voting rights, racial equity, and economic inequality continue to define the unresolved tensions of contemporary American democracy — the issues that the APUSH framework equips students to analyze with historical depth and nuance.
    SOCGEOWXT
MAGPIES Framework

Thematic Breakdown

P
Politics & Power (PCE)
Conservative revolution vs. liberal response, 1980–present
▼

Period 9's political story is the triumph, limits, and contested legacy of the conservative revolution Reagan launched in 1980. Reagan's three core commitments — tax cuts, deregulation, and anti-Communism — fundamentally reshaped both the Republican Party and the terms of American political debate. Even Democratic Presidents Clinton and Obama operated within the constraints Reagan's revolution had established. The end of the Cold War left American foreign policy without a clear organizing principle; the September 11 attacks temporarily produced a rally-around-the-flag consensus that George W. Bush used to justify the Iraq War — a conflict whose costs (financial, military, and moral) deeply damaged both American power and public trust.

Evidence: Reaganomics · Contract with America · NAFTA · Clinton impeachment · PATRIOT Act · Iraq War · Tea Party · ACA · political polarization metrics
E
Economy & Technology (WXT)
Globalization, deindustrialization & inequality
▼

The most consequential economic story of Period 9 is globalization's transformation of the American working class. Free trade agreements (NAFTA, WTO accession for China) moved manufacturing to lower-wage countries, producing wealth at the top and stagnant wages and community collapse for working-class Americans. The digital revolution created extraordinary wealth in coastal knowledge-economy cities while leaving deindustrialized regions of the Rust Belt and South behind. The 2008 financial crisis — caused by deregulated financial markets, predatory lending, and the collapse of a housing bubble — required the largest government bailouts since the Great Depression and produced the worst recession since the 1930s.

Evidence: NAFTA · WTO · deindustrialization of Rust Belt · dot-com boom/bust · 2008 financial crisis · TARP · income inequality data · gig economy
I
American Identity (NAT)
Diversity, immigration, and contested national identity
▼

The United States became significantly more demographically diverse during Period 9. The Immigration Act of 1965's long-term effects produced a dramatic shift in immigration patterns — from predominantly European to Latin American and Asian origins. By 2020, non-Hispanic whites were projected to become a minority of the U.S. population, a transformation that intensified cultural and political debates about national identity. LGBT Americans achieved landmark legal victories — Lawrence v. Texas (2003, decriminalizing sodomy), Obergefell v. Hodges (2015, marriage equality) — while debates over immigration, affirmative action, and racial equity continued to contest the meaning of American identity.

Evidence: Immigration Reform Act 1965 long-term effects · DACA · Obergefell v. Hodges · Black Lives Matter · demographic projections · culture war debates
Historical Thinking Skill

Skill Spotlight: CCOT

Prompt: Analyze continuities and changes in the role of the United States in the world from 1980 to the present.

Continuity
What Persisted?
  • The U.S. remained the world's leading military and economic power throughout
  • American foreign policy continued to reflect both idealistic (democracy promotion) and realist (resource/strategic interests) motivations
  • Domestic debates between interventionism and restraint persisted across administrations
Change
What Changed & When?
  • (1991) Cold War ended, removing the organizing principle of containment from foreign policy
  • (1991) U.S. became the world's sole superpower — the "unipolar moment"
  • (2001) September 11 reoriented foreign policy toward counterterrorism and "War on Terror"
  • (2003–present) Iraq War's failure challenged American military supremacy and global credibility
Model Thesis

"While the United States maintained its position as the world's leading military and economic power from 1980 to the present, its role in the world underwent fundamental transformation — from Cold War superpower constrained by Soviet rivalry to unchallenged hegemon after 1991 to a power humbled by asymmetric warfare, economic competition, and the limits of unilateral force revealed by the Iraq War — demonstrating that American primacy required multilateral cooperation and clear strategic purpose to be effective."

⚠️
Objectivity Note for Period 9
Events in Period 9 are recent enough to feel partisan. The APUSH exam expects you to apply the same historical thinking skills to the Reagan era, the Iraq War, and the 2008 financial crisis as you would to the Civil War or the New Deal. Practice framing these events in terms of causation, continuity and change, and evidence — not political preference.
Primary Source Practice

HIPP Analysis: Reagan's First Inaugural Address (1981)

"In this present crisis, government is not the solution to our problem; government is the problem. From time to time we've been tempted to believe that society has become too complex to be managed by self-rule, that government by an elite group is superior to government for, by, and of the people. Well, if no one among us is capable of governing himself, then who among us has the capacity to govern someone else?… It is my intention to curb the size and influence of the Federal establishment and to demand recognition of the distinction between the powers granted to the Federal Government and those reserved to the States or to the people."

— Ronald Reagan, First Inaugural Address, January 20, 1981  ·  10.8% unemployment; 13% inflation
📋
Document Context
Ronald Reagan (1911–2004) delivered this address as the 40th president during a period of severe economic stagnation (stagflation), the Iran hostage crisis, and widespread public disillusionment with government following Vietnam and Watergate. His election represented the culmination of a thirty-year conservative movement and a decisive rejection of the New Deal liberal consensus that had dominated American politics since 1933.

Key Concept 9.1
A new conservatism grew to prominence in the United States, as liberals responded by refining their own political ideas.

Key Terms
Period 9 Must-Know
Supply-Side Economics"Reaganomics" — cutting top tax rates would stimulate investment and produce growth that "trickled down" to all income levels. Critics called it "trickle-down economics."
NAFTA (1993)North American Free Trade Agreement — eliminated tariffs with Mexico and Canada, accelerating manufacturing movement to lower-wage regions.
PATRIOT Act (2001)Dramatically expanded government surveillance authority post-9/11 — critics argued it violated 4th Amendment protections; a central civil liberties debate of the era.
Obergefell v. Hodges (2015)Supreme Court ruling establishing marriage equality — the culmination of decades of LGBT civil rights activism and litigation.
StagflationThe combination of high inflation and high unemployment that plagued the 1970s — undermined Keynesian economic theory and created the conditions for Reagan's victory.
New Right CoalitionReagan's political coalition: fiscal conservatives, evangelical Christians (Moral Majority), southern whites, and anti-Communist cold warriors — reshaped the Republican Party for 40 years.
Exam Quick Tips
Period 9 = Only 5%
Don't over-invest in Period 9. Know Reagan's ideology, end of Cold War, 9/11 consequences, and 2008 crisis. Context + causation questions dominate — detailed event memorization matters less.
Reagan = Break from New DealFrame Reagan's election as the end of the New Deal liberal consensus that had governed since 1933. His victory is Period 9's most important turning point.
Connect to Earlier PeriodsThe highest APUSH scores come from making cross-period connections. Connect Reagan's states' rights rhetoric to Calhoun/nullification (P4), deregulation to Gilded Age laissez-faire (P6), and the War on Terror's civil liberties debates to the Sedition Acts (P3, P7).
