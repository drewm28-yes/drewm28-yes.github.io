APUSH Historical Period

Period 2

Colonial Society & Imperial Competition

Between the founding of Jamestown in 1607 and the outbreak of the French and Indian War in 1754, European powers established distinct colonial systems in North America that shaped the continent's future. England's thirteen colonies developed regional identities — the theocratic New England settlements, the slave-plantation Chesapeake, and the religiously tolerant Middle Colonies — while simultaneously deepening ties with Britain through the Atlantic economy. Growing tensions between colonial self-governance and imperial control, between enslaved Africans and their enslavers, and between settlers and Indigenous peoples created fault lines that would define the next century of American history.

Key Concepts & Major Events

🏛️
Exam Focus
Period 2 accounts for 10% of the exam. Key DBQ/LEQ themes: comparing colonial regions (New England vs. Chesapeake vs. Middle Colonies), analyzing the causes of Bacon's Rebellion, evaluating the role of the First Great Awakening in creating colonial unity, and the roots of colonial resistance to British authority.
  • 1607
    Jamestown & the Chesapeake Model
    England's first permanent colony survived through tobacco cultivation — a cash crop that created insatiable demand for labor. The headright system gave 50 acres to anyone who paid their own or another's passage, creating a class of large landowners. Initially, indentured servants supplied the labor; by the late 1600s, race-based chattel slavery would replace them. The House of Burgesses (1619) established the first representative assembly in English North America.
    WXTPCESOC
  • 1620
    Plymouth & Puritan New England
    Separatist Pilgrims at Plymouth (1620) and Puritan Massachusetts Bay Colony (1630) established covenant communities grounded in religious obligation. John Winthrop's vision of a "City Upon a Hill" (1630) framed New England as a moral experiment for the world. Town meetings, literacy requirements for Bible reading, and congregational church governance cultivated habits of self-governance that would outlast Puritanism itself.
    SOCNATPCE
  • 1660s
    Navigation Acts & Mercantile Control
    A series of Navigation Acts required colonial goods to be shipped on English vessels and through English ports, embedding the colonies into a mercantilist system designed to benefit the mother country. Salutary neglect — Britain's lax enforcement of these rules through the early 1700s — allowed colonial merchants to develop semi-independent economic practices, creating expectations of autonomy that Parliament would later try to revoke.
    WXTPCEWOR
  • 1676
    Bacon's Rebellion
    Virginia frontier settlers, led by Nathaniel Bacon, rose against Governor Berkeley's government, attacking Native villages and burning Jamestown. The rebellion's multiracial composition — poor white and Black servants fighting together — alarmed Virginia's planter class. The aftermath accelerated the shift from indentured servitude to race-based chattel slavery, as planters concluded enslaved Africans were a safer, more controllable workforce.
    PCESOCNAT
  • 1675–80
    King Philip's War & Pueblo Revolt
    Two major Native American uprisings shook colonial complacency: King Philip's War (Metacom's War) in New England devastated dozens of colonial towns before being suppressed at tremendous cost to both sides; the Pueblo Revolt (1680) in New Mexico drove Spanish colonizers out for over a decade — the most successful Native uprising against European colonization in North American history.
    PCEMIGGEO
  • 1739
    Stono Rebellion & Slave Codes
    Near Charleston, South Carolina, a group of enslaved Africans — many recently arrived from the Kingdom of Kongo — rose up and killed approximately 20 whites before being suppressed. The Stono Rebellion prompted South Carolina to pass harsher Slave Codes restricting enslaved people's movement, assembly, and literacy, demonstrating both the growth of the slave system and the perpetual resistance it provoked.
    SOCPCENAT
  • 1730s–40s
    First Great Awakening
    Itinerant preachers like George Whitefield and Jonathan Edwards ignited a cross-colonial religious revival emphasizing personal conversion, emotional experience, and spiritual equality before God. The Awakening challenged established church hierarchies ("Old Lights" vs. "New Lights"), encouraged independent thinking, created a shared cultural identity across colonial boundaries, and inadvertently prepared colonists for the egalitarian arguments of the coming Revolution.
    SOCNATPCE
MAGPIES Framework

Thematic Breakdown

E
Economy & Technology (WXT)
Regional economies, Atlantic trade, and the roots of slavery
▼

Period 2's most consequential economic story is the rise of chattel slavery. Tobacco in the Chesapeake, rice and indigo in the Carolinas, and sugar in the Caribbean created insatiable demand for coerced labor. The transatlantic slave trade forcibly transported millions of Africans to the Americas, creating the foundations of America's racial economy. The triangular trade linked New England rum, West African slaves, and West Indian molasses into an integrated Atlantic economy that enriched ports from Boston to Bristol.

The Navigation Acts embedded colonists within British mercantile policy, but salutary neglect allowed merchants — especially in New England — to develop semi-independent trade networks that formed the economic backbone of colonial independence arguments. The market economy was also producing a colonial middle class distinct from both the planter aristocracy and the laboring poor.

Evidence to Use: Tobacco & headright system · Triangular trade · Navigation Acts · Salutary neglect · Middle Passage · Plantation economy of the Carolinas
S
Social Structures & Culture (SOC)
Class, religion, and regional identities
▼

Period 2 produced three sharply distinct regional colonial cultures. New England was defined by Puritan religious community, town meeting democracy, and a relatively egalitarian (for white men) social structure. The Chesapeake and Southern colonies developed a planter aristocracy atop a growing enslaved population, with stark class hierarchies. The Middle Colonies — Pennsylvania, New York, New Jersey, Delaware — were characterized by religious pluralism, ethnic diversity, and commercial activity.

The First Great Awakening cut across these regional identities, creating for the first time a shared colonial emotional experience. Its emphasis on spiritual equality challenged both religious hierarchy and, implicitly, social hierarchy — making it a crucial cultural precursor to Revolutionary ideology.

Evidence to Use: Puritan covenant community · Salem Witch Trials (1692) · Quakers and religious tolerance · Anne Hutchinson · First Great Awakening · Bacon's Rebellion class tensions
P
Politics & Power (PCE)
Colonial self-governance vs. imperial control
▼

A central tension of Period 2 is the gradual development of colonial self-governance traditions that would later resist British imperial authority. Colonial assemblies — the Virginia House of Burgesses, the Massachusetts General Court — developed habits of legislating, taxing, and governing that colonists came to view as rights rather than privileges. Salutary neglect allowed these institutions to function with remarkable autonomy for decades.

At the same time, conflicts like Bacon's Rebellion, the Salem Witch Trials, and the Pueblo Revolt demonstrated that political authority was contested at every level — between colonists and governors, between settlers and Native nations, and between enslaved peoples and their enslavers. The Dominion of New England (1686–89), which briefly consolidated northern colonies under a royal governor and abolished elected assemblies, produced such fierce resistance that it was overthrown during the Glorious Revolution.

Evidence to Use: House of Burgesses (1619) · Mayflower Compact · Town meetings · Navigation Acts · Dominion of New England · Glorious Revolution (1688) · Salutary neglect
Historical Thinking Skill

Skill Spotlight: CCOT

Prompt: Analyze continuities and changes in labor systems in the British colonies from 1607 to 1754.

⬌ Continuity
What Persisted?
  • Coerced labor remained the foundation of southern colonial agriculture throughout
  • Tobacco remained the dominant Chesapeake cash crop from 1612 to 1754
  • Racial hierarchy between European colonizers and non-white peoples persisted and deepened
  • Economic extraction for metropolitan (British) benefit remained the colonial system's purpose
→ Change
What Changed & When?
  • (1607–1670s) Indentured servitude dominated Chesapeake labor — mostly white servants
  • (1676) Bacon's Rebellion revealed the danger of a large class of armed, discontented servants
  • (1680s–1700s) Rapid shift to race-based chattel slavery as the preferred labor system
  • (1700s) Slave codes legally entrenched racial hierarchy; Black population surged in South
📝 Model Thesis

"While coerced labor remained central to British colonial economies throughout 1607–1754, the specific form of that labor underwent a decisive transformation after Bacon's Rebellion, as planters replaced the unstable system of white indentured servitude with race-based chattel slavery — a change that permanently embedded racial inequality into American economic and social structures."

Primary Source Practice

HIPP Analysis: John Winthrop, "A Model of Christian Charity" (1630)

"For we must consider that we shall be as a city upon a hill. The eyes of all people are upon us. So that if we shall deal falsely with our God in this work we have undertaken, and so cause Him to withdraw His present help from us, we shall be made a story and a by-word through the world… We must be knit together in this work as one man. We must entertain each other in brotherly affection. We must be willing to abridge ourselves of our superfluities, for the supply of others' necessities… The Lord will be our God, and delight to dwell among us, as His own people, and will command a blessing upon us in all our ways, so that we shall see much more of His wisdom, power, goodness and truth than formerly we have been acquainted with."

— John Winthrop, A Model of Christian Charity, 1630  ·  Delivered aboard the Arabella crossing to Massachusetts Bay
📋
Document Context
John Winthrop (1588–1649) was the first governor of Massachusetts Bay Colony, leading the 1630 Puritan migration of roughly 700 settlers. This sermon, delivered before landing, articulated the Puritan mission as a divine covenant — a collective obligation to build a godly community visible to all. The phrase "city upon a hill" has become one of the most-cited expressions of American exceptionalism in political discourse.

Period 2 · 1607–1754
Key Concept 2.1
Europeans developed varied colonization and migration patterns, influenced by imperial goals, cultures, and environments, competing with each other and Native Americans for resources.
Key Concept 2.2
The British colonies participated in political, social, cultural, and economic exchanges with Britain that encouraged both stronger bonds and resistance to British control.
Key Terms
Period 2 Must-Know
Salutary NeglectBritain's lax enforcement of trade laws, allowing colonial assemblies and merchants to develop autonomous habits of self-governance — critical context for later resistance.
Headright SystemGranted 50 acres to anyone paying passage to Virginia; produced land-owning class and incentivized importation of servants and enslaved people.
Indentured ServitudeLaborers who worked for 4–7 years in exchange for passage; replaced by chattel slavery after Bacon's Rebellion revealed its political dangers.
Triangular TradeAtlantic trade circuit linking rum (New England) → enslaved people (West Africa) → molasses (West Indies) → rum; integrated colonial and imperial economies.
First Great AwakeningReligious revival (1730s–40s) emphasizing personal conversion and spiritual equality; challenged church hierarchy and created first inter-colonial shared identity.
Chattel SlaverySystem treating enslaved people as permanent, inheritable property — legally codified through slave codes after Bacon's Rebellion to replace indentured servitude.
