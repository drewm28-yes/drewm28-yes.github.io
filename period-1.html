APUSH Historical Period

Period 1

Contact, Conquest & Early Colonization

Long before European contact, diverse and complex civilizations thrived across the Americas — from the agricultural Pueblo peoples of the Southwest to the confederacies of the Northeast. The arrival of Columbus in 1492 initiated a catastrophic collision of worlds: European ambitions for wealth, religious conversion, and imperial glory unleashed devastating epidemics, forced labor systems, and cultural erasure upon Indigenous populations. The resulting Columbian Exchange permanently transformed ecology, demography, and economies on both sides of the Atlantic, setting the structural conditions for centuries of exploitation and colonization.

Key Concepts & Major Events

🏛️
Exam Focus
Period 1 accounts for only 5% of the exam and does NOT appear in the DBQ. Focus on SAQ and MCQ contexts. Key skills tested: comparing Native American societies, explaining the Columbian Exchange's consequences, and analyzing competing European colonial models.
  • Pre-
    1492
    Diverse Pre-Contact Native Civilizations
    Across North America, Native peoples developed sophisticated societies shaped by their environments: the agricultural Pueblo peoples of the Southwest, the nomadic Great Plains nations following buffalo herds, the Iroquois Confederacy of the Northeast, and the maize-cultivating Mississippi Valley cultures. These societies were not static — they traded, warred, and adapted continuously.
    MIGGEOSOC
  • 1492
    Columbus Arrives; Columbian Exchange Begins
    Christopher Columbus, sailing for Spain, reached the Caribbean, initiating sustained contact between the Old and New Worlds. The resulting Columbian Exchange moved crops (potatoes, maize, tomatoes westward; wheat, sugar, horses eastward), animals, and — most catastrophically — diseases. European pathogens like smallpox killed up to 90% of some Indigenous populations, fundamentally reshaping the demographic landscape.
    GEOWXTMIG
  • 1494
    Treaty of Tordesillas
    Spain and Portugal divided the non-European world between themselves along a line of longitude, with Spain taking most of the Americas. This agreement formalized European assumptions that Indigenous peoples had no sovereignty over their own territories — a legal and moral framework that underpinned centuries of conquest.
    WORPCE
  • 1512
    Encomienda System Established
    Spain formalized the encomienda, a labor tribute system granting Spanish colonizers (encomenderos) the right to demand labor and tribute from Indigenous peoples in exchange for "protection" and Christian instruction. It produced a rigid racial caste system — peninsulares, criollos, mestizos, mulattos, Indigenous, and African — that structured colonial Latin American society for centuries.
    WXTSOCPCE
  • 1519–21
    Conquest of the Aztec Empire
    Hernán Cortés, exploiting internal political divisions and the devastating impact of smallpox, conquered the Aztec (Mexica) Empire at Tenochtitlán. Similarly, Francisco Pizarro destroyed the Inca Empire (1532). These conquests demonstrated the lethal combination of European military technology, disease, and political manipulation — and produced enormous wealth that funded further Spanish expansion.
    WORPCEGEO
  • 1550s
    Bartolomé de las Casas & the Black Legend
    Spanish friar Bartolomé de las Casas documented and condemned the brutal treatment of Indigenous peoples under Spanish colonialism in his A Short Account of the Destruction of the Indies (1542). His writings contributed to the "Black Legend" — a critique of Spanish colonialism used by rival European powers to justify their own colonial ambitions as more humane.
    SOCNAT
  • 1565–1607
    European Colonization Expands: St. Augustine to Jamestown
    Spain founded St. Augustine, Florida (1565), the oldest permanent European settlement in North America. France established fur-trade networks through the Great Lakes. England's failed Roanoke Colony (1587) and the founding of Jamestown (1607) — funded by the Virginia Company, a joint-stock company — launched sustained English colonization, driven by profit rather than purely religious motives.
    WXTMIGWOR
MAGPIES Framework

Thematic Breakdown

G
Geography & Environment (GEO)
How environment shaped Native and colonial societies
▼

Geography was destiny in Period 1. The diverse environmental zones of North America — arid Southwest, forested Northeast, fertile river valleys — produced radically different Indigenous societies. The Pueblo peoples built permanent adobe settlements around irrigated maize agriculture; Great Plains nations developed nomadic cultures following bison herds; Eastern Woodlands peoples built complex confederacies and traded along river networks.

European colonizers' geographic ambitions collided with these existing landscapes. Spanish colonizers were drawn to regions with mineral wealth (silver mines of Mexico and Peru); French colonizers followed fur trade networks along rivers; English colonizers sought fertile agricultural land on the Atlantic seaboard. The Columbian Exchange itself was fundamentally a geographic story — the movement of species, diseases, and peoples across oceanic barriers.

Evidence to Use: Pueblo cliff dwellings · Iroquois Confederacy river-based trade · Spanish silver mines of Potosí · French fur trade along the St. Lawrence · Columbian Exchange ecology
A
America in the World (WOR)
European imperial competition & global trade
▼

Period 1 must be understood within the context of European imperial rivalry. Spain, Portugal, France, the Netherlands, and England all competed for colonial territory, trade routes, and resources. Mercantilism — the economic theory that national wealth depended on accumulating gold and silver through favorable trade balances — drove states to extract colonial resources as aggressively as possible.

The Treaty of Tordesillas (1494) demonstrated that European powers treated the Americas as territory to be partitioned among themselves, without any regard for Indigenous sovereignty. Each imperial power developed different colonial strategies: Spain relied on conquest and forced labor; France prioritized trade alliances and intermarriage with Native nations; England initially used joint-stock companies and indentured labor.

Evidence to Use: Treaty of Tordesillas · Spanish encomienda system · French fur trade alliances · Dutch New Amsterdam · English Virginia Company joint-stock model
S
Social Structures & Culture (SOC)
Race, religion, and cultural collision
▼

Contact between Europeans, Native Americans, and Africans produced profound social disruption and new hierarchies. Spanish colonialism created a rigid casta system based on racial mixture and ancestry. The Catholic Church played a dual role — both condemning Indigenous brutality (Las Casas) and serving as an instrument of cultural erasure through forced conversion and mission systems.

The arrival of enslaved Africans — the first ship reached the Americas in 1525 — introduced a third population into this collision of worlds. The foundations of race-based chattel slavery were being constructed in this period, rooted in economic need and justified by emerging racial ideologies.

Evidence to Use: Bartolomé de las Casas · Spanish casta system · California missions · Catholic conversion efforts · 1525 African slave trade to Americas
Historical Thinking Skill

Skill Spotlight: CCOT

Prompt: Analyze continuities and changes in Native American societies as a result of European contact from 1492 to 1607.

⬌ Continuity
What Persisted?
  • Many Native nations maintained political independence through strategic alliances
  • Indigenous agricultural practices (maize, beans, squash) continued in most regions
  • Native trade networks persisted, though increasingly incorporating European goods
  • Spiritual and cultural practices survived, often blended with imposed Christianity
→ Change
What Changed & When?
  • (1492–1520s) Epidemic disease collapsed many Indigenous populations by 50–90%
  • (1519–1532) Spanish conquest destroyed Aztec and Inca empires
  • (1512+) Encomienda forced Indigenous labor into colonial economic systems
  • (1550s+) Forced religious conversion dismantled traditional spiritual structures
📝 Model Thesis

"While Indigenous peoples maintained elements of political autonomy and cultural identity in regions distant from Spanish conquest, European contact fundamentally transformed Native American societies through the introduction of epidemic diseases, the imposition of forced labor systems, and the destruction of political sovereignty — changes that varied significantly by region and colonial power involved."

Primary Source Practice

HIPP Analysis: Bartolomé de las Casas (1542)

"It was upon these gentle lambs… that from the very first day they clapped eyes on them the Spanish fell like ravening wolves upon the fold, or like tigers and savage lions who have not eaten meat for days. The pattern established at the outset has remained unchanged to this day, and the Spaniards still do nothing save tear the natives to shreds, murder them and inflict upon them untold misery, suffering and distress, tormenting, harassing and persecuting them mercilessly… The reason the Christians have murdered on such a vast scale and killed anyone and everyone in their way is purely and simply greed."

— Bartolomé de las Casas, A Short Account of the Destruction of the Indies, 1542  ·  Written to King Charles I of Spain
📋
Document Context
Bartolomé de las Casas (1484–1566) was a Spanish Dominican friar who had witnessed Spanish colonization firsthand in the Caribbean. A former encomendero himself, he underwent a moral conversion and became the most prominent European critic of Spanish colonial violence. His writings were addressed to the Spanish crown in an attempt to reform colonial policy.

CB Key Concepts
Period 1 · 1491–1607
Key Concept 1.1
As native populations migrated and settled across North America, they developed distinct and complex societies by adapting to diverse environments.
Key Concept 1.2
Contact among Europeans, Native Americans, and Africans resulted in the Columbian Exchange and significant social, cultural, and political changes on both sides of the Atlantic.
Key Concept 1.3
Europeans developed varied colonization patterns, influenced by imperial goals, cultures, and environments, and competed with each other and Native peoples for resources.
Key Terms
Period 1 Must-Know
Columbian ExchangeThe transfer of plants, animals, diseases, and people between Old and New Worlds after 1492 — the most consequential ecological event in human history.
EncomiendaSpanish forced-labor system granting colonizers the right to demand tribute and labor from Indigenous peoples — justified as "Christian protection."
Casta SystemSpanish colonial racial hierarchy based on ancestry and degree of European, Indigenous, and African "blood" — codified social inequality.
Joint-Stock CompanyBusiness model spreading financial risk across investors; used by English (Virginia Company) and Dutch to fund colonization.
MercantilismEconomic theory that national wealth depended on accumulating gold/silver through trade surpluses; drove colonial resource extraction.
Iroquois ConfederacyMilitary and political alliance of five (later six) northeastern nations — a sophisticated federal structure predating the U.S. Constitution.
